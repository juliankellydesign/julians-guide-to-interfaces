Principle № 001

Everythingserves a purpose

Every interface decision should communicate something or help someone complete an action.

This does not require minimalism. Detail is useful when it has a clear communication role.

01Core principle

Every element has a job

For every decision, ask “What is this doing?”

A shift in font weight can establish hierarchy. Space can show whether elements belong together. A shadow can explain elevation. Animation can show continuity or cause and effect.

Use as many visual elements as the interface requires. Each additional element should contribute information.

Process

Change altitude, hold the user’s voice, earn the simple result

How I approach the work itself: jump between the super narrow view and the super wide view—make close to the pixels, edit from above. Know which of a product’s three voices is yours. And explore the crazy stuff, because simple, obvious solutions are earned by everything that was tried on the way.

AltitudeMake low, edit high

Stay close to the pixels while making; pull up to ask whether it works and who it serves; zoom back in and adjust. Harsh on the work in critique, gentle on yourself while making—ideas are fragile.

Three voicesYou are the voice of the user

Engineering asks how it gets built. Product asks how it serves the business. Design asks how it becomes the best experience for the people using it. The tension between them is healthy.

ExplorationSimplicity is earned

“Least effort for the best result” is the business voice talking. Try the crazy versions and show them to people—the simple, obvious answer is a conclusion of exploration, even when it was the first thing you tried.

Warning signWhen the exploration stops, that’s burnout, not efficiency

03.1Color foundation

Build gray and color scales perceptually

Use 13 stops from pure white to near-black

Use stops 0, 50, 100–900, 950, and 1000. Stop 0 is pure white. Stop 1000 performs the role of black but is never pure black. Use the OKLCH curve as a starting model, then judge the rendered differences on actual displays.

The six base color scales—blue, red, orange, yellow, purple, and green—reuse the same stops and endpoints, hold one hue constant, and shape chroma with a cubic Bézier arch clamped to the sRGB gamut. They share their own lightness curve, which holds midtones lighter than the gray curve because sRGB chroma peaks near L 0.6–0.75. Equal stops share equal lightness across all six scales.

Lightness curvesgray (.35, .2, .55, .6)color (.5, .3, .6, .5)
Chroma archchroma = 0.16 × 4t(1 − t), clamped to 95% of sRGB

04Rhythm

Use space to show relationships

The distance between elements communicates whether they belong together

Use a 2px grid for values through 20px and a 4px grid for larger values. Take working tokens from the spacing ladder, which doubles its step as sizes grow. Keep repeated relationships consistent, then compensate optically when geometric alignment looks wrong.

Term

Gestalt means form or shape

Gestalt is a German word and a common design term for how people perceive separate elements as related groups or as a whole. Spacing is one of the clearest ways to communicate those relationships.

Visual weight

Concentrate visual weight by importance

Start a design by ranking importance: the most important thing, then the second, then the third. The most important element gets decisively more visual weight—an order of magnitude, not an increment. Size, color, and motion are all sources of weight that add up: a small but brightly colored button can be the loudest element on screen, while a large surface carries weight through area alone and keeps its other sources quiet. Maxing every source on one element leaves it nothing to play against—balance the composition, with white space as a counterweight of its own.

RuleMost real estate, quietest voice; least real estate, loudest voice

Balancing alignment

One right-aligned element fixes a left-heavy layout

In conversational AI interfaces, the user’s message sits in a bubble and the AI’s reply floats as free text. When a reply needs to render a card with controls, full width would be too much card—but left aligned it can look really left-heavy. The fix is simple: a single thing aligned to the right balances the composition, and it’s often the chat bubble that’s already there.

RuleOne counterweight is enough—often it’s the chat bubble

Scrolling layers

Offset the padding of scrolling layers

When content scrolls under a fixed element, the two layers should not share the exact same edge padding. On a 402px-wide device, search results padded 16px scroll under nav circles inset 16px—the edges perfectly hit, and the accidental join reads as designed, then breaks. Offset one layer decisively and judge it in motion. Within a single plane, shared edges remain the rule.

Adding a style

Ask twice before adding a style

A new style gets two questions. The first looks backward and reuses an already-taught pattern; the second designs the addition as a type that earns its future uses. Attention shadows, icon sizes, and color meanings all enforce the same underlying rule in their own disciplines.

01 / BackwardHave I seen something similar elsewhere in this interface?

Reuse it and strengthen the pattern.

02 / ForwardCan the new thing be repurposed elsewhere?

Design it as a type, not a one-off.

RuleTreatments bind to types, never to instances

Margins and padding

Keep padding consistent

Padding is part of a component’s identity: the same card keeps the same interior space on every screen, so its rhythm and keylines hold. The change in available space belongs to margins and layout—gutters compress and columns drop while the inside of components stays put. The same consistency runs across components: two elements of the same size share the same padding, so their contents land on the same keylines.

RuleMargins absorb the screen; padding belongs to the component—and to the size

Sizing rhythm

Size even unrelated elements to one rhythm

An avatar, a button, and a card thumbnail may never sit in the same group today, but interfaces get rearranged. When sizes already share a rhythm, seemingly unrelated elements fall onto the same keylines the moment they meet—aligning them later is much easier. Sizing to the rhythm is an investment in layouts that don’t exist yet.

RuleElements that share a sizing rhythm align themselves later

Interrogate the ask

Every element earns its spot

An ask arrives at some altitude—“design a chat interface for this bot” prescribes the interface; “add AI to my product” prescribes almost nothing. Step back and work the chain: what is the value, what capabilities does it give users—the things they want done by the system—and only then, what surface serves them best. A prescribed interface is one candidate. And when a conventional interface gains novel elements, each one earns its spot with proof; a pattern never gets in because it’s shorthand for the technology behind it.

01 / Ask“Add AI” · “Make it a chat”

Whatever the altitude, don’t start drawing yet.

02 / ValueWhat is this actually adding?

If the capability is weak, fix the capability—don’t dress it in a novel interface.

03 / CapabilitiesWhat do users want done?

Name the things the system can now do for people.

04 / InterfaceWhat surfaces them best?

Maybe the prescribed one. Maybe not. Then keep asking at lower altitudes.

Worked exampleLet search be search

A product wanted AI-powered search, and the first guess was a chat interface for it—chat as shorthand for AI. Users trying to search got something not-quite-like search, slower, with results that weren’t much better. The honest fix: make search good, AI-powered if needed. Then let conversational elements in one at a time as each proves itself—reply-to-refine first, no bubbles, no prose; generative text separately; a real chat only where chat is natural, like asking about the thing you’re already looking at.

RuleReuse what people understand; make every departure demonstrate its value

Shadows

Use shadows as a tool

Shadows are a tool, not a theme—never all-or-nothing. Hold one meaning per shadow everywhere it applies and leave everything else flat; strokes follow the same selective consistency. A shadow must also read apart from its container: 20% black around a container at 20% lightness lands on the container’s own color, and the interface goes muddy. Tonality can be painterly, but hard edges keep the definition—when the two blend, ask whether the shadow is needed at all.

Token scaffold

Five sizes, three styles

Consistent sizing keeps elements on shared keylines and out of each other’s way; consistent color keeps meaning attached to emphasis. My button scaffold: heights 24, 32, 40, 48, 56—XS through XL—and Primary, Secondary, Tertiary styles. The finite ranges are the alarm: needing a sixth size or a Quaternary style means too many variants. On web and desktop the heights get nudged to fit the screen—a nudge changes a value, never adds a role. A slotted-in design system overrules these values.

RuleNeeding Quaternary means you’re too far out in the woods

Musing

Nesting buttons in inputs

An open lean, not a settled principle: I often wonder whether the right move is to nest a button inside an input or have it exist outside. Buttons are best nested inside inputs when they take action on the content within. Nesting is the strongest grouping claim an interface can make—placing a button inside the input’s boundary says this button acts on this content—so a button whose action reaches beyond the input stays outside.

MusingNested means “this button acts on this content”

06Imagery

Relate icons to type by function

Icons sit between type and image, and between content and interface

An icon used as interface shorthand should remain visibly distinct from text. A caret, chevron, or arrow used to complete a phrase behaves more like punctuation and should align closely with the typography.

Icon sizes

Mostly 20, tapering off

Icon sizes follow a tapering frequency: mostly one workhorse size, a few in a second, one or two in a third. I use 20 for most icons, sprinkle in 16 and 24, and keep 12 and 32 rare. An icon that accompanies text derives its size from that text and holds the pairing everywhere—a 20 icon always goes with 16 text—unless it’s a chevron or arrow being tuned optically against its phrase.

Continuous transitions

Continuously transition only between elements that do the same thing

A continuous transition communicates that an element is the same—it is just moving, growing, or changing form. The floating chip and the reply bar are one object: tapping either begins a reply that refines the search, so the chip becomes the bar. The tab bar does something different, so no transition connects it to the composer—it makes room by moving out of the way, staying itself the whole time. The tab bar and the composer are the same size, so static designs could never explain how one replaces the other; the chip’s change in size is what makes the transition legible.

Refine search

RuleThe chip becomes the composer because they do the same thing; the tab bar just moves out of the way

Transition levels

Animate the level that changes

A tab switch fades through with a small x translation, giving sibling tabs a spatial relationship. The search bar searches the whole app, so it does not fade or move when the tab changes—it sits at a level above the content, just like the tab bar. What moves reads as content; what stays reads as structure.

RuleThe search bar doesn’t move because it doesn’t belong to the tab

Press states

Give controls a juicy press state

A press state is the interface acknowledging contact, and it is so often overlooked. It matters most on touch, where there is no hover—press is the only feedback between intent and result, and a control without one feels dead under the finger. The response is immediate; the juice comes from the quality of the motion, not its duration.
