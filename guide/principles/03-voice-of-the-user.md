# Be the voice of the user

## In brief

A product is typically built by three archetypes. Engineering is the voice of how it will be built. Product—a PM, a founder—is the voice of the business: how the work serves the business goals. The designer is the voice of the user: given this thing we want, how do we make it the best possible experience for the people who use it? These voices are often in opposition, and that is a good thing. Tension between them is healthy.

## Three voices

- **Engineering** — how will we build this?
- **Product** — how will this work toward our business goals?
- **Design** — how will this be the best experience for the people who use it?

Each voice is a role, not a person; small teams collapse them into fewer people, but the questions still all need asking. The designer's question is the one that goes unasked when no one holds it deliberately.

## Know which voice is talking

"What is the least we can do to get the best result" is the voice of the business—someone looking at what the work costs and minimizing it. That is a legitimate voice, but it is not yours. The designer's version is: what are users actually interested in? That might be a crazy thing, and it should still be explored.

Holding your voice does not mean winning every argument. It means the user's side of the tension is reliably present when the three voices pull against each other.

## Working rules

- Hold the user's question—how is this the best experience for the people using it?—as your primary job.
- Treat opposition between engineering, business, and user voices as healthy tension, not a problem to eliminate.
- Notice when cost-minimizing framing is speaking through you, and return to the user's framing.
